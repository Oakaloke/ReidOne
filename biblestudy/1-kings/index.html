1 Kings

From Solomon's glorious temple to a kingdom torn in two, 1 Kings traces the cost of unfaithfulness and the courage of God's prophets.

← All 66 Books

Overview

First Kings opens with the aging David and a struggle over succession, which ends with Solomon established on the throne. Solomon asks God not for riches or long life but for wisdom to govern God's people, and the Lord grants him wisdom and far more. His famous judgment between two women claiming one child displays his God-given discernment, and his reign brings unprecedented peace, wealth, and international renown to Israel during a golden age of the united monarchy.

The crowning achievement of Solomon's reign is the building of the temple in Jerusalem, a magnificent house for the worship of the Lord. At its dedication Solomon prays a great prayer, and the glory of God fills the temple. Yet even in his splendor, the seeds of decline are sown. Solomon's many foreign wives turn his heart toward their gods, and his idolatry provokes the Lord, who announces that the kingdom will be torn from his son, though not entirely, for David's sake.

After Solomon's death the kingdom splits in two. His son Rehoboam, refusing wise counsel, drives the northern tribes to rebel under Jeroboam, who sets up golden calves at Bethel and Dan to keep his people from worshiping in Jerusalem. From this point the book follows two lines of kings, Israel in the north and Judah in the south, evaluating each by whether they followed the Lord. Most do evil, and the spiritual decline accelerates, especially in the north.

The latter chapters spotlight the prophet Elijah, who confronts the wicked King Ahab and his queen Jezebel and their promotion of Baal worship. Elijah announces a drought, is fed by ravens and a widow, and triumphs dramatically over the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel, where fire falls from heaven. Worn out and afraid, he meets God not in wind, earthquake, or fire but in a gentle whisper, and is recommissioned. The book ends with Ahab's continued wickedness and approaching judgment.

Context at a Glance

Author
Anonymous; tradition associates Jeremiah
Written
Events around 970-850 BC; compiled later
Genre
Historical narrative
Audience
Israel and Judah, reflecting on the monarchy and exile
Central theme
Faithfulness to God determines the fortunes of king and kingdom

Key Verse

1 Kings 3:9 (WEB)

Give your servant therefore an understanding heart to judge your people, that I may discern between good and evil; for who is able to judge this your great people?”

Solomon's request for an understanding heart to discern good and evil shows what God delights to give those who seek wisdom over self.

The Big Movements

  • Solomon established (chs 1-4) — Solomon secures the throne and asks God for wisdom to rule.
  • The temple built (chs 5-8) — Solomon constructs and dedicates the temple as God's glory fills it.
  • Solomon's decline (chs 9-11) — Wealth and foreign wives turn Solomon's heart to idolatry.
  • The kingdom divides (chs 12-16) — Israel splits north and south, and kings lead the people into sin.
  • Elijah and Ahab (chs 17-19) — Elijah confronts Baal worship and meets God in a gentle whisper.
  • Ahab's wars and end (chs 20-22) — Ahab's conflicts and judgment close the book.

Key Figures

  • Solomon — David's son, granted great wisdom, who builds the temple but later turns to idolatry.
  • Rehoboam — Solomon's son whose harsh folly splits the kingdom.
  • Jeroboam — First king of the northern tribes who leads Israel into calf worship.
  • Elijah — The bold prophet who confronts Baal worship and trusts God against the powers of his day.
  • Ahab — A wicked northern king who, with Jezebel, promotes idolatry in Israel.

Pointing to Christ

Solomon's wisdom, temple, and peaceful reign foreshadow a greater Son of David, yet his failures expose the need for a flawless King. Jesus is the true temple where God dwells with his people, the one greater than Solomon, and the King whose wisdom and faithfulness never falter.

Big Lessons

  • God honors those who seek wisdom to serve others rather than self.
  • Outward success cannot protect a heart that drifts from God.
  • Compromise in worship corrupts and divides God's people.
  • Leaders shape the spiritual health of those they lead.
  • God meets the weary and afraid with gentleness and fresh purpose.
  • Faithfulness, not splendor, is the true measure of a life.
  1. What does Solomon's prayer for wisdom reveal about godly priorities?
  2. How did Solomon's heart drift even amid great blessing, and how can we guard ours?
  3. What does the division of the kingdom teach about the consequences of pride and folly?
  4. How does Elijah's stand on Mount Carmel encourage you to trust God against the odds?
  5. Why is it significant that God met Elijah in a gentle whisper?
  6. In what ways is Jesus greater than Solomon and his temple?
