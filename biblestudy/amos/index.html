Bible Study · Minor Prophets

Amos

A herdsman from Judah thunders against the rich and complacent of Israel, demanding that justice and righteousness flow like a river.

← All 66 Books

Overview

Amos was no professional prophet but a shepherd and tender of sycamore figs, called by God to leave Judah and confront the wealthy northern kingdom of Israel. It was a time of peace and prosperity, but beneath the surface lay deep corruption: the rich exploited the poor, justice was bought and sold, and worship had become a hollow show. Into this complacency Amos brought a roaring, uncompromising word.

He begins by pronouncing judgment on Israel's neighbors, drawing his hearers in with approval, before suddenly turning the spotlight on Israel and Judah themselves. Their privilege as God's chosen people was not a shield but a greater responsibility: 'You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities.' Election meant accountability.

At the heart of Amos is his fierce critique of injustice and false worship. The people trampled the needy, took bribes, and lounged in luxury while ignoring the ruin around them, yet they kept their feasts and offerings as if all were well. God says he hates their hypocritical festivals and longs instead for justice to roll down like waters and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.

Amos sees visions of coming judgment, locusts, fire, a plumb line, a basket of summer fruit, that show Israel is ripe for ruin. Yet the book does not end in darkness. After the threat of exile, God promises to raise up the fallen tent of David, restore his people, and plant them again in their land, never to be uprooted. Judgment serves a deeper purpose of restoration.

Context at a Glance

Author
Amos, a shepherd and fig-grower from Tekoa
Written
Around 760-750 BC, during the reign of Jeroboam II
Genre
Prophecy (Minor Prophet)
Audience
The prosperous northern kingdom of Israel
Central theme
Justice, righteousness, and judgment on social and religious corruption

Key Verse

Amos 5:24 (WEB)

But let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream.

God's longing is not for sacrifices and songs but for justice and righteousness to flow continually through his people's lives.

The Big Movements

  • Judgment on the nations (chs. 1-2) — God indicts surrounding nations, then turns the charge against Judah and Israel.
  • Words against Israel (chs. 3-4) — Privilege brings accountability; past warnings went unheeded.
  • A call to seek the LORD (ch. 5) — Amos pleads for justice and true worship rather than empty ritual.
  • Woes on the complacent (ch. 6) — Those at ease in luxury face the coming judgment.
  • Visions of judgment (7:1-9:10) — Locusts, fire, a plumb line, and summer fruit picture Israel's ripeness for ruin.
  • Promise of restoration (9:11-15) — God will rebuild David's fallen tent and plant his people securely.

Key Figures

  • The LORD — The God of all nations who roars against injustice yet promises to restore his people.
  • Amos — A shepherd from Tekoa called to deliver God's word to Israel despite opposition.
  • Amaziah — The priest of Bethel who opposes Amos and tries to silence and expel him.
  • The wealthy of Israel — The complacent rich who exploit the poor while keeping up religious appearances.
  • The poor and needy — Those crushed by injustice, for whom God's heart and judgment are stirred.

Pointing to Christ

Amos longs for true justice and righteousness, which find their fullness in Jesus, who proclaimed good news to the poor and embodied perfect righteousness. The promise to restore David's fallen tent is quoted by James in Acts as fulfilled in Christ and the gathering of the nations into his kingdom.

Big Lessons

  • Worship without justice is offensive to God.
  • Privilege increases responsibility before God.
  • God cares deeply about how the vulnerable are treated.
  • Prosperity can breed a dangerous spiritual complacency.
  • True religion shows itself in justice and righteousness, not just ritual.
  • God's judgment ultimately serves his purpose of restoration.
  1. Where might 'justice and righteousness' be missing in my community or my own life?
  2. How can worship become hollow, and how do we keep it genuine?
  3. In what ways does comfort or prosperity dull our concern for others?
  4. How does Amos challenge the idea that being God's people means escaping accountability?
  5. What does it look like to let justice 'roll down like waters' in practical terms?
  6. How does the closing promise of restoration give hope after such hard warnings?
