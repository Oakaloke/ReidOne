Kind, Tenderhearted, Forgiving

A short study of Ephesians 4:32

“Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you.”

Ephesians 4:32 (ESV)

Some verses are so familiar we stop hearing them. Ephesians 4:32 is one of them — short enough to cross-stitch onto a pillow, easy to nod at and move past. But sit with it for a few minutes and it turns out to be one of the most demanding, and most hopeful, sentences in the New Testament. It tells us how to treat the people who are hardest to love, and then it tells us where the strength to do it comes from.

Who wrote it, and when

Ephesians was written by the apostle Paul, most likely from prison around AD 60–62, probably during his house arrest in Rome (Ephesians 3:1; 4:1; 6:20). Interestingly, several of the earliest manuscripts don’t include the words “in Ephesus” in Ephesians 1:1, which has led many to read the book as a circular letter — a message meant to be carried from church to church across the province of Roman Asia. That fits the tone: it is warm and pastoral, but it rarely addresses one congregation’s specific quarrels.

The letter has a clean shape. The first three chapters describe what God has done for us in Christ — chosen, redeemed, forgiven, raised, and joined together into one new people. The last three chapters describe how that reality reshapes ordinary life — how we speak, work, marry, parent, and forgive. Chapters 1–3 are the roots; chapters 4–6 are the fruit. Ephesians 4:32 sits right at the hinge where doctrine becomes doorstep.

Where the verse sits

From 4:17 onward, Paul is working out one big picture: because you belong to Christ, “put off your old self” and “put on the new self” (Ephesians 4:22–24). He gets specific. Stop lying and tell the truth (4:25). Don’t let anger fester (4:26–27). Stop stealing and start working so you have something to give (4:28). Then, just before our verse, he lists what has to go: “Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice” (Ephesians 4:31).

That’s the “put off.” Verse 32 is the “put on.” And this is the part we usually miss: Paul does not stop at “don’t be bitter.” He gives us something to become instead. Kindness is not simply the absence of malice; forgiveness is not merely the absence of a grudge. The empty space left by anger has to be filled with something, or the anger comes back. Verse 32 is the replacement, not just the prohibition.

What the words mean

Three commands, each rich in the original Greek:

Chrēstos — “be kind.” This is kindness with its sleeves rolled up: goodness that is useful, gracious, and actually helpful to the person in front of you. It’s the same word Jesus uses when he says his yoke is “easy” (Matthew 11:30). Kindness here isn’t a soft feeling; it’s active good done for someone else’s sake.

Eusplanchnos — “tenderhearted.” The root points to the inner organs, the gut. It describes a compassion you feel in your body before you decide it in your mind — the ache that moves you toward someone who is hurting. Paul is asking for softened hearts, not just polished manners.

Charizomai — “forgiving one another.” This verb is built on the word for grace (charis). To forgive, biblically, is not to settle a debt down to the penny; it is to give something away — to release a claim you had every right to hold. Forgiveness is an act of grace, not a negotiated payment.

The Holy Spirit connection

Notice what comes only two verses earlier: “Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption” (Ephesians 4:30). Paul frames all of this — the honesty, the cooled anger, the kindness, the forgiveness — as either grieving or gladdening the Spirit who lives in us. Bitterness grieves him; kindness keeps step with him.

That points us to Galatians 5:22–23, where kindness is literally named as part of “the fruit of the Spirit.” And fruit is the key word. You don’t clench your teeth and manufacture an apple; a healthy tree grows one. In the same way, the kindness and forgiveness of Ephesians 4:32 are not raw willpower we grind out on a bad day. They are what the Spirit grows in a life connected to Christ. Our part is to abide, to stop grieving him, and to cooperate — and his part is to produce the fruit.

The ground of the command

Everything turns on the last clause: “as God in Christ forgave you.” That little word as is the engine of the whole verse. We do not forgive in order to earn grace; we forgive because we have already received an ocean of it. The measure and the motive are the same: the mercy God has shown us at the cross.

Jesus told a whole parable about this in Matthew 18:21–35 — a servant forgiven an unpayable fortune who then seizes a fellow servant by the throat over pocket change. The point lands hard: forgiven people forgive. Paul says the same thing to another church almost word for word: “forgiving each other; as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive” (Colossians 3:13). Christian forgiveness always flows out of grace already received, never uphill toward grace we hope to earn.

What God wants from us here

  1. Trade, don’t just delete. Put off bitterness by putting on kindness. Leaving the space empty invites the old anger back.
  2. Feel, then act. Ask God for a tender heart toward the very people you’d rather stay hard toward.
  3. Forgive as grace, not as math. Release the debt the way it was released for you — freely, and more than seven times.
  4. Depend on the Spirit. Stop treating obedience as self-improvement. Ask the One who lives in you to grow what you cannot manufacture.

Living it out

This verse was written for Tuesdays, not just for pulpits. A few concrete places to start:

  • At home: when a family member snaps, choose one kind, useful action before you choose a comeback — a refilled cup, a softened tone, a “let’s try that again.”
  • At work: when a colleague gets credit that was yours, refuse to let it curdle into slander at the coffee machine. Speak well of them instead.
  • At church: when someone has hurt or disappointed you, go to them first, kindly and privately, rather than to everyone else about them.
  • Online: before you post the cutting reply, ask whether it is kind, tenderhearted, and true. If it fails all three, let it go unsent.
  • In your own heart: name the person you find hardest to forgive, and pray for them by name today — grace is often given while we pray.

For discussion

  1. Paul replaces bitterness with kindness rather than just removing it. Where do you need to “put on” something new, not only stop something old?
  2. Which is harder for you right now — being kind, being tenderhearted, or forgiving? Why?
  3. How does remembering “as God in Christ forgave you” change the way you approach someone who has wronged you?
  4. What would it look like this week to depend on the Spirit for this instead of relying on your own willpower?

A closing prayer

Father, thank you that in Christ you have forgiven me a debt I could never repay. Soften my heart where it has grown hard. By your Spirit, grow in me a kindness that is useful, a compassion that moves me toward others, and a forgiveness that gives grace away as freely as you gave it to me. Keep me from grieving your Spirit today, and let the people around me meet your mercy in the way I treat them. In Jesus’ name, amen.
